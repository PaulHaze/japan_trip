# PART 1 — TOKYO (19–27 November)

Unchanged in structure, but a couple of things are worth revisiting.

## Base — LOCKED IN

### 🏨 JR-East Hotel Mets Premier Gotanda — 5 nights, Sun 22 to Fri 27 November

- **Address:** 1-26-3 Higashi-Gotanda, Shinagawa-ku, Tokyo 141-0022
- **Phone:** +81 3-6408-0011
- **One minute from JR Gotanda Station**, Central Exit (east side)
- **Check-in 15:00 / check-out 11:00**

Good call — this is the right base, and the logistics fall out of it very neatly.

**Sunday 22 Nov, Kamata → Gotanda.** One train, no changes. The **Tokyu Ikegami Line** runs direct from Tokyu Kamata to Gotanda — about 20 minutes, and easy with suitcases. Note that Tokyu Kamata is a separate building from JR Kamata; they're adjacent but you walk between them.

**Friday 27 Nov, Gotanda → Shinjuku for the Azusa.** Eight minutes on the Yamanote, then you're at the departure station. You're on the **10:00 Azusa**, so leave the hotel around 09:40 — comfortably inside the 11:00 checkout with no rush at all. Drop the big bags with the front desk for takkyūbin before you go (see Part 4).

**Haneda.** You now fly home out of Kyoto via Itami, so this isn't your departure route — but worth knowing Gotanda is on the **Toei Asakusa Line**, which through-runs onto the Keikyu, putting Haneda 25–30 minutes away if you need it mid-stay.

### What's on your doorstep

**The station lanes.** Gotanda's drinking quarter is under and beside the tracks on the west side — standing bars, yakitori, small izakaya, and absolutely nothing trying to impress you. This is the scruffiness you wanted from Kōenji, and it's a two-minute walk from your room.

**Togoshi-Ginza**, two stops down the Ikegami Line. Tokyo's longest shopping street — 1.3 km of covered and open arcade, about 400 shops, croquettes and old sweet stalls, and almost entirely locals. **Togoshi-Ginza Onsen** is there too: a proper neighbourhood sentō with genuine black *kuroyu* hot-spring water, about 20 minutes' walk or one stop and a short walk from the hotel. Worth an evening.

**Musashi-Koyama**, one further, has an even longer covered arcade (Palm) that stays dry whatever the weather.

**Nakameguro and Yutenji** are a few minutes on the Tokyu lines — the Meguro River walk, and Nakameguro's vinyl and vintage.

**Kōenji is still on** — Yamanote to Shinjuku, then the Chūō, about 25 minutes total. Make it an afternoon-into-evening trip: vintage shops from around 1pm, then the alleys after dark.

## Two additions for your wife's interests

Both of her interests are, slightly awkwardly, better served in **Tokyo** than Kyoto. You have eight nights there, so use them.

**Ōmiya Bonsai Village, Saitama** — this is the one. About 35 minutes from central Tokyo (Toro or Ōmiya-kōen station). After the 1923 earthquake, Tokyo's bonsai growers relocated here as a group and their nurseries are still working today — five or six of them, free to walk into, on quiet suburban streets. The **Ōmiya Bonsai Art Museum** anchors it with a proper curated collection and English signage. Half a day, and it's the single best bonsai experience available to a visitor in Japan. Closed Thursdays.

**The Japanese Sword Museum (NBTHK), Ryōgoku** — the national institution for *nihontō*, with rotating displays of masterpiece blades, mostly Important Cultural Properties and National Treasures. This is a serious museum, not a tourist attraction: it's about the steel, the grain, the *hamon*. It's what a katana enthusiast actually wants to see. See the Ryōgoku day below.

## Sumo — read this first

**There is no sumo tournament in Tokyo during your trip.** The six annual *honbasho* rotate: Tokyo's Ryōgoku Kokugikan hosts January, May and September. The November tournament is the **Kyushu Basho, 8–22 November 2026, at the Fukuoka Kokusai Center**, tickets on sale from 19 September. The Kokugikan will be dark for your whole stay.

Options, in order of how much I'd recommend them:

**Morning practice (*asa-geiko*) at a Ryōgoku stable.** The best substitute, and arguably better than a tournament day. You kneel on the floor a few metres from wrestlers grinding through training, in near-total silence — no crowd, no ceremony, no commentary. **Only possible after 22 November**, because until then every wrestler is in Fukuoka; and it needs to be before the December regional tour. Your window is 24–27 November, which is perfect. Book through a guide or agency — most stables won't admit unaccompanied foreign visitors, and the etiquette is strict.

**Fly to Fukuoka.** You land on 19 November, day 12 of the basho, when the championship is being fought out. Haneda–Fukuoka is about 1h50. Friday 20 November (day 13) works as a long day trip or an overnight. The catch: the final days sell out within hours of release, pre-orders open around mid-August, and it cuts across your Kamata nights. Only if you want the real thing badly enough to rearrange for it.

**A chanko-and-sumo show** with retired wrestlers, in Ryōgoku or Asakusa, runs year-round. Not authentic, but you see bouts up close and eat well. **Asakusa Sumo Club** is the pick — see the Asakusa day below.

**Free alternative worth knowing:** **Arashio-beya**, a stable in Nihonbashi, lets you watch morning training through its street-level windows for nothing, no booking, walk-ins accepted. Check their calendar first as they're often away or closed. Good backup if the guided stable visit falls through.

## Thu 19 – Sat 21 November — Kamata, unplanned

Staggered arrivals: your wife lands Thursday night, you land **20:30 Friday** after the long-haul. Saturday is a write-off by design. No itinerary, no bookings, no early starts. Eat locally, sleep, walk it off.

Kamata rewards low-effort wandering anyway — there's a good scruffy izakaya scene around the east exit, and **Kamata Onsen** and **Hasunuma Onsen** are proper black *kuroyu* bathhouses within walking distance. That's about the right level of ambition for a decompression day.

*(One casualty worth noting: Tori-no-Ichi, the Edo lucky-rake fair, falls on 19 November this year and 2026 has no third rooster day. It'd have been superb, but not on a landing night.)*

## 📍 Ryōgoku day — Wednesday 25 November

Chosen deliberately. **23 November is Labour Thanksgiving Day**, a Monday public holiday, so most Tokyo museums push their Monday closure to Tuesday the 24th — Wednesday dodges that. The **Edo-Tokyo Museum reopened on 31 March 2026** after a four-year renovation, so Ryōgoku's biggest draw is back after years of being unavailable — but it needs two hours-plus of its own. Either swap it in for the Hokusai museum below, or give it a separate half-day.

**Limited Express Azusa, Shinjuku → Matsumoto.** Direct, no changes, around **2 hours 35 minutes**. Roughly one an hour through the day.

Suggested departures:

|---|---|---|
| Azusa 5 | 08:00 | 10:37 |
| Azusa 9 | 09:00 | 11:39 |

**Decided: the 10:00 (Azusa 13), arriving Matsumoto 12:37.** It fits an 11:00 checkout without any clock-watching — leave the hotel around 09:40, eight minutes on the Yamanote, and you're on the platform with room to spare. You'll still be sitting down to a soba lunch before 13:15 with the whole afternoon ahead of you.

**Three things to know:**

- **Every seat on the Azusa is reserved — there are no unreserved cars.** Book ahead. This is a Friday in peak autumn-colour season, and the Azusa sells out a week or more in advance in season. Book online via the JR East site or Navitime and collect with the QR code at a machine.
- Getting to Shinjuku from Gotanda is eight minutes on the Yamanote. Some Azusa services start from Tokyo Station instead — check which yours does.
- **Sit on the left-hand side.** Mt Fuji shows up around Ōtsuki and across the Kōfu basin, and later the Southern Alps come up on the same side approaching Kobuchizawa. In late November with clear winter air, it's a genuinely good ride.

Base fare plus limited express charge is around ¥6,600 per person.

## Where to stay in Matsumoto

You want to be near the station — it's a 15-minute walk from the station to the castle, so "near the station" and "near everything" are more or less the same thing in Matsumoto.

**My pick: Dormy Inn Matsumoto (Natural Hot Spring).** Practically visible from the station exit. Rooms are small and plain — it's a business hotel and doesn't pretend otherwise — but it has a **rooftop natural hot-spring bath**, which after a travel day and before a big walking day is exactly what you want. Free ramen at night, laundry room, and it sits in the middle of the restaurant streets. Around ¥15,000–20,000 for two.

**For more character: Onyado Nono Matsumoto.** Same group as the Dormy Inn but their tatami brand — you take your shoes off at the door and the entire hotel is matted underfoot. Natural hot spring, near the station. It feels considerably more Japanese than a standard business hotel for not much more money.

**For real character: Matsumoto Hotel Kagetsu.** Established 1887, Matsumoto's oldest, and furnished throughout in local **Matsumoto mingei** folk-craft furniture — the town has a serious craft tradition and this is the best place to sleep inside it. It's about 5 minutes from the castle, so 12–15 from the station rather than 2. If you want one memorable stay rather than one convenient one, this is it.

**Also solid:** Richmond Hotel Matsumoto and Matsumoto Marunouchi Hotel (both reliable, both central). **Nunoya Ryokan** on Nakamachi-dōri is a small family-run inn inside a converted storehouse if you'd rather do tatami and futon.

# PART 3 — MATSUMOTO (Friday afternoon + all Saturday)

You've gained a full Saturday here, so this is no longer a rushed stopover.

## Friday afternoon and evening

You're on the ground at 12:37 and the sun goes down at about **16:30**, so this is a short afternoon — roughly four hours of daylight. That's fine: everything below is compact and the castle is Saturday's job anyway.

**13:00 — drop bags, then lunch: soba.** Shinshū (Nagano) is Japan's soba heartland and Matsumoto takes it seriously. Zaru soba with the buckwheat-cooking-water *sobayu* at the end. Most soba specialists here close mid-afternoon, so eat before you wander.

**14:00 — Nakamachi-dōri.** The old merchant street — white-walled *namako-kabe* storehouses with black-and-white lattice plasterwork, now craft shops, coffee roasters, galleries and sake dealers. This is where Matsumoto's mingei folk-craft tradition lives. Quiet, browsable, no crowds.

**15:15 — Nawate-dōri**, running along the Metoba River a couple of minutes north — livelier and sillier, full of frog statues and frog-themed shops (there's a local river-frog legend), plus antique dealers and snack stalls. **Yohashira Shrine** sits at the end of it.

**16:00 — the castle at dusk.** Don't go in — that's tomorrow, and the keep stops admitting people late afternoon. But the **grounds and moat are free and open**, and the black keep reflected in the water as the light goes is the best view of it you'll get. Five minutes from Nawate. It's a good way to close the afternoon.

**Evening.** Matsumoto's drinking quarter is the warren of tiny bars around **Ise-machi** and the lanes off Ōte. Nagano is one of Japan's great sake prefectures and the ratio of good sake to tourists here is excellent. Ask at your hotel for the local *jizake* — Nagano rice, Alpine water.

## Saturday — the full day

**08:30, Matsumoto Castle, at opening.** One of only five castles in Japan designated National Treasures, and the keep is *original* — 1590s, never burned, never rebuilt in concrete. Black lacquered boards, six floors, and staircases so steep they're closer to ladders. Inside the keep is the **Teppō Gura**, a serious collection of matchlock firearms, armour and arms — which is where your wife's samurai interest gets its first proper feed. Go at opening; by 10am there's a queue for the keep and it's one-in-one-out on the stairs.

**Former Kaichi School**, 5 minutes' walk. Japan's oldest surviving Western-style school building, 1876, with an octagonal clock tower — a Japanese carpenter's attempt at Western architecture using entirely Japanese techniques, and the result is wonderfully strange. Designated a National Treasure in 2019 and **reopened in 2024 after a long restoration** (older guidebooks say it's closed — that's out of date).

**Lunch on Nakamachi**, then pick one or two of:

- **Matsumoto City Museum of Art** — Yayoi Kusama was born here, and the permanent Kusama installations are the reason to go. The polka-dot exterior is visible from the street.
- **Japan Ukiyo-e Museum** — the Sakai family collection, one of the largest private ukiyo-e holdings in the world (100,000+ prints, rotating). It's west of town, so a taxi or bus.
- **Ishii Miso** — traditional wooden-vat miso brewery, free tours, and you'll smell it before you see it.
- **Matsumoto Timepiece Museum** — small, charming, near Nakamachi.

**Late afternoon: Asama Onsen.** About 15 minutes by bus north-east of the centre, a hot-spring quarter with over a thousand years of history that has never become a resort. The public bathhouse (Hot Plaza Asama) is cheap and completely local. A soak at 4pm as it gets dark, then back into town for dinner, is a very good way to spend the end of a day.

**Getting around:** the Town Sneaker loop bus is ¥200 a ride or ¥500 for a day pass and links station–castle–Nakamachi. But honestly, everything above is walkable for you two.

**Daylight warning:** sunset in Matsumoto in late November is around **16:30**. Front-load the day.

# PART 4 — MATSUMOTO → KYOTO VIA NARAI (Sunday 29 November)

This is the day that replaces the Kiso Valley drive. You still get Narai — the finest of the eleven Kiso post towns and the one I'd have picked if you could only do one — and you go straight through to Kyoto after.

## The route

Narai is on the JR Chūō Main Line, and **limited express trains do not stop there** — only locals do. So the shape is: local down to Narai, explore, local onward to Kiso-Fukushima, then pick up the limited express there and run south to Nagoya, then Shinkansen east to Kyoto.

| Leg | Train | Approx. duration |
|---|---|---|
| Matsumoto → Shiojiri → **Narai** | Local (Chūō Main Line), one change at Shiojiri | 45–55 min |
| *Narai — explore* | | *2.5–3 hours* |
| Narai → **Kiso-Fukushima** | Local | ~25 min |
| Kiso-Fukushima → **Nagoya** | Ltd Exp **Shinano** | ~1 hr 20 |
| Nagoya → **Kyoto** | Tōkaidō Shinkansen (Hikari or Nozomi) | ~35 min |

**Indicative timings:** leave Matsumoto around 08:15–08:30, be in Narai by about 09:15, leave Narai around 12:30–13:00, Nagoya mid-afternoon, **Kyoto around 16:30–17:00.** That gets you checked in with a whole evening in hand.

**Cost:** roughly ¥12,500–13,500 per person all up.

**Important caveat:** the local trains through the Kiso are **sparse — sometimes only one an hour, occasionally with a two-hour gap.** The times above are the right shape but you must check the actual timetable for 29 November on Jorudan or Navitime and build the day around the real departures rather than the other way round. Reserve the Shinano and the Shinkansen; the locals are unreserved, just turn up.

## The luggage problem — and the fix

Narai station is tiny. It has **no proper coin lockers** — the station office will hold bags for about ¥300 each until roughly 16:00, and there are a couple of small lockers a minute's walk away, but nothing designed for two large suitcases.

**Decided: forward the big bags from Tokyo, not Matsumoto.** Hand the suitcases to the **Mets Premier Gotanda front desk on Friday morning 27 November** and send them by **takkyūbin** (Yamato) straight to the Kyoto hotel. Say "takkyūbin onegaishimasu" and give them the Kyoto hotel's name and address — it's completely routine.

- **Cost:** roughly ¥1,650 for a Size 100 bag up to about ¥2,530 for a full Size 160 one, Tokyo to Kyoto. Call it ¥5,000 for the pair.
- **Timing:** dropped before lunch Friday, delivered by 18:00 Saturday — a day before you check in. Either **ask the Kyoto hotel in advance to hold them** (normal, but get a yes in writing, especially if it's a small machiya), or have the Gotanda desk write **Sunday 29 November** in the requested-delivery-date field on the waybill. Put your name and check-in date on the slip either way.
- **You travel with a large cabin case each** for Friday, Saturday and Sunday, plus a daypack. Hotels supply toiletries, nightwear and towels, and the Dormy Inn has a coin laundry if you want it.
- **Keep the cold-weather gear with you, not in the shipped bags.** Matsumoto is 0–8°C and Narai is higher and colder again. Coats, thermals, gloves and proper shoes travel on your back.

This means **no luggage handling in Matsumoto at all**, and in Narai you'll be walking the post town with a daypack — which is how it should be done. Leave the two cabin cases at Narai station's office (about ¥300 a bag, until roughly 16:00) rather than wheeling them over the uneven stone street.

**No return leg to Tokyo.** The bags stay with you in Kyoto until Saturday 5 December, when they go to Itami and on to Haneda. See Part 5 for the departure-day options, including forwarding them ahead to the airport.

## Narai-juku

The 34th of the 69 post towns on the Nakasendō, and the wealthiest of them — "Narai of a Thousand Houses." Its preserved street runs **over a kilometre**, longer than Tsumago or Magome, with a mountain wall at either end. Because it's harder to reach than the southern post towns and most tour buses skip it, it feels lived-in rather than curated.

What to look for:

- The **mizuba** — roadside spring-water wells that watered travellers and horses, still running.
- **Chōsen-ji temple** and its painted dragon ceiling.
- **Nakamura House** — a merchant's house you can go inside, showing how the lacquer trade actually worked.
- **Kiso lacquerware** (*Kiso-shikki*) — this is the town's craft and has been for centuries. The shops here are the real thing, not souvenir stock.
- **Kiso no Ōhashi**, the big arched wooden bridge just north of the station.

Lunch on the street — *gohei-mochi* (grilled rice skewers in walnut-miso), soba, and *oyaki* dumplings.

Late November means bare trees, wood smoke and mountain cold. Dress for it — the Kiso valley is high and it will be several degrees colder than Kyoto.

**Bike hire: Wednesday and Friday only.** Two days, not four — three of your days are train or foot days. Ask about a two-day rate, or just hire Wed–Fri and keep them over Thursday for convenience.

## The timing is in your favour

Kyoto's colour peaks from about 20 November into the first week of December, and in warmer years the central temple gardens are stronger in early December than late November. **The extra day pushes you deeper into that window, not out of it.**

The mountains will be past it — Ōhara, Takao and Kurama peak mid-November and will be bare. Don't build a day around them. Everything below leans on the late-peak sites instead: Shimogamo's **Tadasu no Mori** (peaks early-to-mid December), Tōfuku-ji, Kennin-ji, the Botanical Gardens. Crowds also thin noticeably from 1 December, so five of your six days are on the good side of that line.

## The bikes

Kyoto is a flat grid in a valley basin, and with e-bikes the north-eastern hills stop mattering.

**Where to hire:** **Kyoto Ocean** (Saiin/Tanbaguchi) has the biggest batteries and lends a charger for ¥500 extra. **KCTP** has multiple branches and includes insurance — but e-bikes must go back to the branch you hired from. **Kyoto Eco Trip** (Kyoto Station Hachijō exit) has seven bike types and its own rider's map. **Kyoto Option** was recommended by a rider on r/JapanTravelTips.

**On bike size:** expect Japanese *mamachari* proportions. If either of you is tall, ask about frame size and consider the sports or touring models.

**Rules — Kyoto enforces this properly:**

1. **Never leave a bike outside a designated parking area.** Impounding is aggressive, recovery is ~¥3,500 and half a day.
2. **Some streets ban bicycles outright** — parts of Kawaramachi-dōri, around City Hall, and the Karasuma-to-Yasaka corridor. Route around them.
3. **No phone in hand, no headphones, lights on after dark.** New fine schedules came in during 2026.
4. **Never ride on temple or shrine grounds.** Dismount at the gate.
5. **Temples mostly have their own bike parking**, a couple of hundred yen. Use it.
6. **The Kamo River path** is the traffic-free spine of the city.
7. **Take the shop's paper map** — riders rate it above any app.
8. **Riding after drinking is a criminal offence**, properly enforced. Bikes get parked before dinner, every night.

## Monday 30 November — Fushimi: the mountain, then the sake ⭐

### Morning — Mount Inari

| Time | |
|---|---|
| 06:30 | Leave. Sunrise is around 07:00 in early December. |
| 06:50 | **Fushimi Inari Taisha** — open 24 hours, free, torii tunnels lit. Arriving in the half-dark is the best version of this place. |
| 07:00 | **Climb.** 30–45 min to **Yotsutsuji**, where the city view opens and the path splits into the summit loop. |
| 08:00 | **Do the full loop.** Past Yotsutsuji the torii thin out and almost everyone turns back — which is why the upper mountain is worth it. 4.5 km, 180 m ascent, ~2 hours total. |
| 09:30 | Down. Breakfast on the shrine approach — grilled quail, inari-zushi, tsujiura senbei. |

⚠️ Check the noticeboard — part of the route beyond the main shrine was closed as of mid-2026. Probably reopened by December, but look before committing.

### Afternoon — the sake district

Keihan south to **Chūshojima**. White-walled *sakagura* along willow-lined canals.

**Why Fushimi tastes different:** originally written 伏水, "hidden water," for the soft groundwater beneath it. Soft water gives a rounder, gentler sake — historically *onnazake* — against the drier, sharper *otokozake* from Kobe's hard-water Nada. Fushimi is **Japan's second-largest brewing district**, around twenty breweries still working, and **Gekkeikan has been here since 1637.**

- **Gekkeikan Ōkura Sake Museum** — a working brewery's museum in a *sakagura*, old wooden tools, tasting at the end.
- **Teradaya** ⚔️ — the inn where **Sakamoto Ryōma** was ambushed in 1866 and escaped through a window with a sword wound, preserved with the marks in the woodwork. Three minutes from the breweries.
- **Kizakura Kappa Country** — second brewery, museum, garden, beer hall.
- **Fushimi Yume Hyakushū** — the visitor centre, pouring flights across the whole district. Where to compare them side by side.
- **Jikkokubune canal boats** — the flat-bottomed boats that ran rice and sake down the Yodo to Osaka. Autumn season usually ends early December; **verify the last date.**

**Also underfoot:** the **Battle of Toba-Fushimi**, January 1868 — the opening engagement of the Boshin War that finished the shogunate. The sake warehouses and the end of the samurai class occupy the same streets.

### Evening — sake and yakitori

**Torisei** 鳥せい — yakitori in an old brewery building belonging to **Yamamoto Honke**, pouring their own sake straight from the tanks next door. The correct answer to "sake and yakitori in Fushimi."

Keihan back to Gion-Shijō, about 15 minutes. No bikes to worry about.

**🍶 Seasonal bonus:** late November into December starts the brewing year, so you may find ***shiboritate*** — new-pressed, unpasteurised sake that doesn't travel and is basically only drinkable where it's made.

## Tuesday 1 December — recovery

Deliberately soft. Flat, walkable, downtown, no schedule. You will have earned it.

- **Kyoto Gyoen** (Imperial Palace Park) — vast gravel avenues and huge old trees, so big it never feels busy. Ten minutes' walk from downtown.
- **Nishiki Market** — go early, before the midday crush. Standing snacks the length of the arcade.
- **Teramachi and Shinkyōgoku arcades** — covered, so weatherproof, and this is Kyoto's vintage and record territory: **100000t Alonetoco** (secondhand books and records), **Meditations** (upstairs, leftfield), plus the vintage clothing around Sanjō.
- **Kennin-ji** — Kyoto's oldest Zen temple, right at the edge of Gion, twin dragons on the ceiling. Holds its colour into mid-December.
- **A sentō in the afternoon** — the correct hangover treatment.
- **Inoda Coffee, Sanjō honten** — 1940, wood panelling, waistcoated staff. An institution rather than a good coffee shop, and better for it.

**Evening:** this is a good night for **Yoramu**, the aged-and-obscure sake bar — you're not riding, and it's central. Or hold it for later in the week if the thought of sake is still unwelcome.

## Wednesday 2 December — bonsai and the north-west 🌲

**First bike day.** Collect them in the morning.

**Optional early start:** **Tōfuku-ji at opening (08:30)** — early December is exactly when the Tsūten-kyō bridge maples peak, and you'll get half an hour of near-solitude. The Mirei Shigemori Hōjō gardens — modernist checkerboard moss — are extraordinary and always emptier than the bridge. **Sennyū-ji**, the Imperial mortuary temple, is right beside it and almost never visited. *(Or leave both for Saturday morning.)*

**Daitoku-ji.** A vast walled Zen complex — more a monastic town than a temple, two dozen sub-temples inside, only a handful open. Quiet, because no single famous photograph pulls people to it.

- 🌲 **Hōshun-in — the Kyoto Bonsai Garden.** Opened 2021 inside the complex, designed and run by **Seiji Morimae**, displaying museum-grade bonsai including *kichō bonsai* — formally registered masterpiece trees — in a proper Kyoto garden rather than on trestle tables. The best bonsai viewing in Kyoto by a distance. **Appointment only — arrange this now.**
- **Kōtō-in** — a bamboo-and-maple approach path that's one of the great autumn sights in the city, and nothing like as busy as it deserves.
- **Zuihō-in** and **Daisen-in** for dry-landscape gardens.

**Lunch:** *shōjin ryōri* at **Izusen** inside Daiji-in, served in stacked red lacquer bowls in the temple garden. Or **Ichimonjiya Wasuke** by Imamiya Shrine — charcoal-grilled aburi-mochi, reputedly the oldest continuously operating restaurant on earth, founded around the year 1000. Its rival **Kazariya** faces it across the lane, and has only been going since 1637.

**Afternoon: Nishijin.** The weaving district — source of *Nishijin-ori* brocade for a thousand years. Machiya lanes where you can still hear looms through the walls. **Orinasukan** is a small working weaving museum in an old workshop.

**Then north to Kamigamo.** Up the Kamo path to **Kamigamo Shrine** — Kyoto's oldest, UNESCO-listed, almost always empty. Outside its gates is **Shake-machi**, a preserved street of Shinto priests' houses along a canal, with willows and stone bridges. Very few visitors find it.

**Evening: Funaoka Onsen** — a 1923 bathhouse with carved wooden transoms, majolica tilework and an outdoor bath. A working neighbourhood sentō, not an attraction, and magnificent. **Sarasa Nishijin**, a café in a converted 1930s bathhouse with the original tiling, is round the corner.

## Thursday 3 December — the katana day ⚔️

**No bikes** — this is a train day west.

### Morning: forge a blade

**Masahiro Tantōjō sword forge, Kameoka.** A licensed, practising swordsmith about 40 minutes west of Kyoto. Over roughly four hours you work *tamahagane* — the same steel used for real katana — and forge a 15 cm **kogatana**, a hilt-less blade in katana form, which you keep. The smith teaches the history and metallurgy as you go.

- **Runs Tue, Thu, Sat, Sun** — Thursday works.
- **Take the 09:00 session**, finishing around 13:00.
- **¥38,000 per person.** Not cheap, but it's the real thing rather than a costume experience.
- Kyoto Station → San-in Line to Chiyokawa (~25 min), then Keihan Kyoto Kōtsū bus to Honume, 6 minutes' walk.
- **Book well ahead** — small operation, limited places.

### Afternoon: Arashiyama on the way home ⭐

**Kameoka is one stop up the San-in Line from Arashiyama**, so this now costs you nothing. Get off at Saga-Arashiyama around 13:30 and take the quiet end of it, away from the bamboo crush:

- **Saga-Toriimoto** — a preserved thatched-roof street on the slope above the tourist strip.
- **Otagi Nenbutsu-ji** — 1,200 stone *rakan* carved by amateurs in the 1980s, every face different, most of them funny. Almost nobody goes.
- **Gio-ji** — a small moss garden, exquisite in late autumn.
- **Ōkōchi Sansō** — the film star's villa and garden, with the best view in Arashiyama and an entry fee that keeps it calm.
- **Togetsukyō bridge at sunset**, from the **south side** — riders and locals both name this as the view. Sunset is about **16:45**.

**Dinner option:** if you're doing **Kitcho Arashiyama**, this is the night for it — you're already there, six minutes' walk from the bridge, and it's closed Wednesdays anyway. Otherwise train back and eat downtown.

## Friday 4 December — the north-east, then the send-off 🍁

**Second bike day.** The best colour day of the week.

**Ride north up the Kamo path** to the Demachiyanagi delta, where the Takano and Kamo meet at a Y with turtle- and heron-shaped stepping stones across the water. One of the nicest ordinary places in Kyoto.

**Shimogamo Shrine and Tadasu no Mori.** The shrine is UNESCO-listed and older than the city, but the reason to come now is the forest: **Tadasu no Mori**, a surviving fragment of the primeval woodland that stood here before Kyoto existed, never cleared, with a stream through it. Its maples are **late** — normally peaking early-to-mid December — so by the 4th they should be turning beautifully, green through orange to red all at once in gradation. **My single strongest recommendation for your dates.**

**Kyoto Botanical Gardens**, just north. Japan's oldest public botanical garden, huge, almost entirely locals. There's a **standing bonsai collection** — not Hōshun-in's level, but a good large display of pines, maples and wisteria, free with garden entry. The **Nakaragi-no-michi** riverside path is a famous late-colour spot.

**Lunch and books in Ichijōji.** Kyoto's ramen street — **Men-ya Gokkei** is the headliner, Tabelog Top 100 five years running, broth so thick a spoon stands in it. **Keibunsha** is one of the most beautiful bookshops in the world.

**Then up the hill** — where the e-bikes pay for themselves:

- **Enkō-ji** — hillside maples, astonishing in autumn, with a view over the whole city from the bamboo above.
- **Shisen-dō** — a poet-scholar's retreat from 1641, white sand garden, a *shishi-odoshi* clacking, and a veranda you're meant to sit on for a long time.
- **Manshu-in** — larger, imperial, superb dry garden.
- **Konpuku-ji** — tiny, with Bashō's hut and Buson's grave behind.

**Ride back down** via **Demachi Masugata**, the old covered arcade. **Demachi Futaba** has been making *mame-mochi* since 1899.

**Return the bikes.**

> ### ✈️ The departure — decided: FLY
>
> **Kyoto has no airport of its own.** The two that serve it are Itami (domestic, ~36 km, simplest bus link) and Kansai (international, much further out). For a Haneda connection, **Itami is the right one.**

>
> ⚠️ **If you can't get both legs on one booking reference, reconsider.** Some airlines will through-check a separately ticketed same-day connection if you ask at the counter, but it's discretionary and you lose the protected connection. Without it, the train is the better option.

# PART 7 — IF YOU DO GET A CAR FOR A DAY

You said maybe. If you do, don't use it for anywhere the train already reaches well. The best use of a Kyoto day-hire car is:

**Miyama Kayabuki-no-Sato** — a village of nearly 40 thatched *kayabuki* farmhouses in the mountains about 90 minutes north-west of Kyoto, still lived in and farmed rather than preserved as a museum. It is the closest thing to the Shirakawa-gō experience you lost when the road trip went, and it's considerably less visited. Drive out through the Kitayama cedar forests, which are beautiful in themselves. In early December there may well be snow on the roofs, which is when it looks best. There's essentially no public transport that makes this work as a day trip, so this is genuinely what a car is for.

Second choice: **the west shore of Lake Biwa** — Ōmi-Maiko, Shirahige Shrine's torii standing in the water, Sakamoto's stone-walled temple district and Hiyoshi Taisha. About an hour out.

If you *don't* get a car, both **Nara** and **Uji** are easy, excellent train day trips — Uji especially, for the tea and Byōdō-in, and it's small enough to do at half pace.

# SEASONAL NOTES

- **Sunset:** ~16:35 in Matsumoto, ~16:45 in Kyoto. Short days. Start early, and plan for dinner to begin in the dark.
- **Temperature:** Matsumoto will be cold — 0–8°C, possibly below freezing overnight, possible snow on the surrounding peaks. Narai is higher and colder still. Kyoto is milder, roughly 4–13°C, but the basin gets a damp cold that bites. Layers, and gloves for the bikes.
- **Colour:** should be very good in Kyoto for your dates, and the plan leans on the late-peak spots deliberately. The mountain sites north of the city will be past it — don't build a day around Ōhara or Takao.
- **Crowds:** heavy through 30 November, noticeably thinner from 1 December. Your last three Kyoto days are the good ones.
- **Nippon Bonsai Taikan-ten** — one of Japan's largest bonsai exhibitions, held annually in Kyoto in late November. In recent years it has run roughly 21–25 November, which would put it just before your Kyoto arrival — but the dates shift year to year and it's worth checking the 2026 schedule. If it happens to run to the end of November, reroute for it; it's a much bigger deal than anything else bonsai-related in the city.
